Chapter 0

Before you start

Claude is very capable and very fast, and it starts every conversation knowing nothing about you. Everything in this guide is about fixing that once, properly, so you never explain your business twice.

Context is the leverageWhat Claude knows about you decides how good its work is. Chapter 1 is the one most people skip, and the one that matters most.
It remembers because you tell it toTwo phrases, "note that" and "wrap up", turn every session into a better next session.
The payoff is a skillA skill is a written method for one job. Once one exists, that job is done the same careful way every time, without you.
Nothing is lost, and you stay in chargeEvery change to the brain is logged on GitHub and can be undone. Claude prepares, drafts and checks. You send, sign, pay and decide.

Before you put your business into this, you should know where it goes and who can look at it. The short version: your brain is yours, the recipe books are mine, and nothing flows from you to me.

  • Everything in it: your brand, your voice notes, your clients, your memory, and every skill you build.

Mine, and what they do

This is the chapter that decides everything after it. Claude's output can only be as good as what it knows about you. You are going to put your brand, your best work, your voice and your clients where Claude will find them every time, and then let it read the lot and write its own notes.

Check the voice against a real sample, not a draft
  1. Ask Claude to write a short email to a client in your voice about something real.
  2. Read it next to one of your own. If it is punchier, warmer or more clever than you are, say so plainly and paste another real email of yours. Do not describe the problem; show it.
  3. Repeat until a client would not notice the difference.
The most common mistake: accepting a voice that is a better writer than you. It reads as marketing to everyone who knows you. A real sample fixes it in one round; feedback in words takes five.

Chapter 3

The habit: note that, and wrap up

Everything you have set up only gets better if what you learn in a session makes it back onto the shelf. Two phrases do that. You never decide what to save or where it goes; Claude does, and tells you.

During a session: note that…

When you tell Claude something it should always know, start with "note that". "Note that Future Proof invoices on the 1st, not month end." It goes straight to the right file, and Claude confirms where.

At the end: type wrap up

1 · YouType "wrap up"When you have finished a piece of work.
2 · ClaudeKeeps or dropsKeeps what will matter again: corrections, decisions, facts about a client. Drops one-offs.
3 · ClaudeFiles it on the right shelfA client fact goes to that client. A way you like things done goes to the rulebook. Today's thread goes to memory.
4 · ClaudeSaves it to GitHub and tells youA short list of what it kept. Logged under your name, undoable. Next session starts with all of it.
What you'll see afterwards Saved for next time
  • Rapid Teams: proposals go to Angus first, never straight to the client (clients/rapid-teams)
  • You prefer proposals under two pages with the price on page one (CLAUDE.md)
  • Open thread: the Vector pitch deck is half done, section 3 next (memory/2026-11-11)
Not kept: today's draft email and the numbers you were checking (one-offs).
Wrap up when you…
  • corrected Claude ("no, we always…")
  • made a decision ("from now on…")
  • told it something about a client
  • left something half done
Don't worry about…
  • wrapping up a quick question: if there is nothing to keep, Claude says so
  • getting it wrong: every change is logged on GitHub and can be undone, and every file is plain text you can read
  • remembering to do it: Claude asks when it hears "thanks, that's it"

What never goes on the shelf

The brain lives on GitHub, private to you today and perhaps shared with a colleague later. Keep these out of it, and Claude will refuse to write them even if you ask.

Bank details. BSB and account numbers, card numbers. Say "the account ending 123".
Passwords and keys. Logins, API keys, tokens. They live in a password manager, never in a file Claude reads.
Tax file numbers and anyone's personal details beyond a name and a role.
Anything on your never-share list. Claude checks it before any file or email leaves.
Chapter 4

Build your first skill

A skill is a written recipe for one job. Once it exists, Claude does that job the same way every time, using everything in your brain, and you only check the result. This is where the set-up pays for itself. Pick one thing you do routinely and get it off your plate.

Pick the job: three questions

1Do you do it every week, the same way?The weekly client update. The proposal after a call. The Friday numbers. The Monday plan. Not "marketing".
2Does it start from something you can hand over?Call notes, an email, a spreadsheet, a transcript, a folder of receipts. If the input is "my head", pick something else first.
3Can you show one you were happy with?One good example teaches more than a page of instructions. If you cannot find one, the job is not ready to hand over yet.

Three yeses: that is your first skill. Two: fine, but expect a second round of corrections. One: pick another.

Two worked examples

Working in the business: call notes to proposal
  • Input: your rough notes or the transcript from a discovery call.
  • Output: a two-page proposal as a Word file, your brand, your structure, your pricing, your voice.
  • Uses: brand, standards, voice, the price list, the client folder.
  • You do: read it once, change what you would change, send.
Working on the business: the Friday page
  • Input: nothing. It reads your accounting system, email and calendar.
  • Output: one page: cash in and out this week, who owes you, what you promised, three things for Monday.
  • Uses: connectors from chapter 2 and every client folder.
  • You do: read it with a coffee. Correct anything wrong with "note that".

ABuild it

Put your good example in the folder

Drop one finished example of the job (the proposal you were proud of, the update the client liked) onto the bench at bench/best-work/ if it is not already there, or tell Claude where it lives if that tool is connected.

Describe the job and let Claude write the recipe
  1. Copy the message below, fill in the brackets in your own words, paste it in.
  2. Claude uses its skill builder to interview you: what comes in, what goes out, what good looks like, where you want to be asked before anything is sent. Answer plainly.
  3. It writes the skill, shows it to you in plain English, and saves it to your brain. Saving into the skills folder brings up one more permission question; allow it.
Build my first skill. The job is [what you do, in one sentence]. It starts from [the input] and ends with [the output]. A good example is in bench/best-work/[file] (or: in [folder] on [SharePoint or Drive]). Use the skill builder, ask me what you need to know one question at a time, and show me the recipe in plain English before you save it.

  1. Give it a real input: this week's call notes, this Friday.
  2. Read the output next to your example. Correct anything in plain words: "too long", "we never quote hourly", "the client's name is wrong".
  3. Run it again. The second run should need fewer corrections than the first.
Wrap up

Your corrections become part of the recipe. From here on, that job is Claude's, and the next skill is easier than this one. Ask Claude: "What else do I do every week that could be a skill?" It has watched you work, and it will have a list.

Every day from here: open the Code tab in your caivman folder, say "note that" when you tell it something that should stick, type "wrap up" when you finish, and run your skill when the job comes round.

Appendix

What is GitHub, actually

Most explanations of GitHub are written by programmers for programmers, which is why it takes everyone else forever and a day to get it. Here is the version for the rest of us. You will not be asked to do anything on it beyond creating a login; Claude does the rest. But it helps to know what the thing is.

Where it came from

In 2005 the people building Linux, the operating system that runs most of the internet, had a problem: thousands of volunteers editing the same pile of code at once. Linus Torvalds wrote Git to solve it. Everyone works on their own copy, every change is recorded with a name and a note, and the copies are merged back together. Where two people changed the same line, it stops and asks instead of guessing. Track changes on steroids, for a whole folder, with nothing ever flattened by "Accept all". GitHub arrived in 2008 as the website that keeps those folders online and adds the social bit: accounts, invitations, who did what.

One consequence worth knowing. Git does its clever line-by-line work on text, and only on text. It will store a PowerPoint, but all it can say about it is "this changed", and it keeps a whole new copy each time. That is why your brain is built from text, and why your decks and spreadsheets stay where they already live.

The one-paragraph version

GitHub is a website that keeps folders. Not like Dropbox, which copies a folder somewhere else and hopes for the best. GitHub keeps every version of every file in the folder, with a note saying who changed what and why, forever. You can look at the folder as it was last Tuesday. You can put any file back the way it was. That is the whole trick, and it is why programmers, who break things for a living, cannot work without it.

Why your brain lives there

HistoryEvery wrap up is a saved version with a note. Six months from now you can see what Claude knew in May.
UndoClaude wrote something wrong into a client file? Put it back. Nothing in the brain is ever truly lost.
A second computerLaptop died, or you want the same brain at home. Sign in, and Claude fetches the lot.
A second personChapter 5. Invite someone, and you both work from the same brain with everyone's changes logged under a name.

What trips people up

It is not Dropbox
  • Dropbox syncs every keystroke, silently, and keeps a month of versions.
  • GitHub saves only when told, with a note, and keeps every version for good.
  • So nothing reaches GitHub until a wrap up. That is deliberate: a save with a reason attached is worth more than a thousand silent ones.
You will barely see it
  • You make a login once and turn on two-factor. That is the last time you visit the website.
  • Everything else, Claude does and tells you about in plain words: "saved to GitHub, three files, logged under your name".
  • If you are ever curious, go to github.com and open your caivman repository. Every file is readable. Every change has a note.
If you only remember one thing: GitHub is where your brain is kept safe, with a memory of every change. Claude does the work; you own the shelf.
